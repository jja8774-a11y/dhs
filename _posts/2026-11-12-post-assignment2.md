# Introduction 

Can Artificial Intelligence (AI) models locate places in an AI-generated story better than humans? In this article, I compare geoannotation data in a map with a human annotated layer, a NER annotated layer, and an LLM layer. The AI-generated story at hand is I the Road by Ross Goodwin, the first AI-generated novel, from 2018. Ultimately, the spatial knowledge and cultural awareness of the human NYU Abu Dhabi annotators surpassed the AI. 

## I the Road by Ross Goodwin 

AI-generated novels were not popular in 2018, and Ross Goodwin was a pioneer in this field. In order to write 1 the Road, he became “the author of the author,” meaning he created an AI to write this book inspired by American road trip stories. At a slightly more primitive time, Goodwin had to connect his Cadillac, Google Maps, a microphone, a rooftop camera, a clock, GPS and his self-made portable AI machine to write captions [(Goodwin, 2018)](https://bombmagazine.org/articles/2018/12/14/ross-goodwins-1-the-road/). As the author of the author, Goodwin constructed this process and story by making the AI tell the story of a road trip from New York to New Orleans. He himself drove from New York to New Orleans, and had the AI machine connected to his Cadillac and his other spatial awareness objects to generate this data and story. I the Road opens the door to early, undeveloped models of AI storytelling, where the storyline is mostly abrupt and senseless; however, this can also be attributed to cuts in the editing. Nowadays, AI may be able to be more coherent in its storytelling. The AI storytelling in Goodwin’s book is confusing, and perhaps the introduction is the only part that makes sense.

## Manual Annotation Journey

As a class we annotated I the Road on Recogito. We were split into groups, and I worked with both Aljood and Joan to annotate Section 6. The purpose of annotation was to generate data on the locations mentioned in the book, and later map it out. We did manual geocoding, a human identification and contextualization of place names found in the text, matching them with possible geographic information using contextual clues. Many of the locations we identified were based on context clues rather than names, given that there were misspellings or inaccuracies. We also used Google Maps and MapQuest to aid our search. For example, in Section 6, three paragraphs in a row mentioned the gas station “Sunoco,” all three on “Franklin Street,” but with slightly different descriptions. Given that we could not find the location on the map in Recogito, we found the latitude and longitude of “Franklin Sunoco” in Alabama and annotated the location three times in the comments. 

When cleaning the data, having the locations in the comments rather than in Recogito’s location fields made the manual annotation data more tedious to process. In order to clean the data, I had to manually move each coordinate from the comments to the LAT and LNG columns, for latitude and longitude. The clean data is available [here]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/recogitoali.csv).

# Three Datasets and the Map 

For this project and its mapping, we used three different data files. The first was the aforementioned manually annotated file, which we worked on in Recogito. The second annotated data file was generated using a rule-based Named Entity Recognition (NER) system on “I, the Road.” Dr. Wrisley used a Python script with geopy for geocoding and spaCy to find likely places. The third data file was also generated by Dr. Wrisley using ChatGPT Sol 5.6, which also used an NER system and geopy.

In order to visualize the data, I used Dr. Wrisley’s notebook in Posit Cloud called “Interactive Leaflet Map with Two Clickable Layers: 1 the Road”. This notebook helped me create a self-contained HTML map with three layers, one each for the manual, NER, and LLM datasets. In order to make the map more visually accessible, I chose a light color palette in ColorBrewer featuring shades of orange, yellow and blue.

# Analysis 

The datasets differed in their counts, including usable and unusable coordinates. The first manual dataset contained 246 rows, 245 mappable and 1 without usable coordinates. The second automatic (spaCy + geopy) dataset contained 341 rows, 248 mappable and 93 without usable coordinates. The third automatic (LLM) dataset contained 374 rows, 281 mappable and 93 without usable coordinates. Meaning, the manually generated data had the fewest mappable coordinates, but also the fewest unusable coordinates. This may be because when manually annotating, it would have been difficult to include the location or latitude/longitude of a place that does not exist. In addition, as I cleaned the data, I made sure no latitudes or longitudes were left in the comments. LLMs are known for hallucinating, and many of the places named in the story were hard to decipher, so the machine may have assigned them to non-existent locations. Interestingly, both automatic datasets had 93 locations without usable coordinates.

The manual annotators were more aware of the route in the story than the AI annotators. The route that the car takes in the story is from New York to New Orleans, with all locations in the US East Coast and South. The first layer of manual data is mostly in the American East Coast and South, except for three points going into the West Coast and Europe. The second and third layers of automated data are fairly similar, with multiple points leaving the East Coast and going into the West Coast, the South, Florida and all continents of the world, with a clear cluster in Europe, and little to no points in Central Africa, Russia, North Africa, and the Middle East. I also noticed the third LLM layer had a few extra outlier points in each continent that it did not share with the second NER layer, including some coordinates in Chile, Egypt, and Italy. 

If I the Road needed a crutch map to be able to follow the story, or a visualization of the road trip, the first layer with the manually annotated data would be the most accurate to follow. If the second or third layers were used to follow the story, they would be misleading for readers. Krupar (2015) notes how maps can have a “strong hold on people's imaginations.” Meaning, the inaccurate automated maps can be convincing for people if not looked at with a clear context of the car’s story.

Some of the places I annotated in Section 6 were not mapped in layers 2 and 3. My portion of the text looked at some Sunoco gas stations and a memorial in Alabama. I thought the AI might annotate the same places; however, we probably differed in our interpretations, as only layer 1 had these. Furthermore, my team did not finish annotating Section 6, but interestingly, a place featured in the text in Section 6 was Fort Deposition of Avalancha. It is most probably somewhere in Alabama, but the LLM annotated it as a coordinate called Avalancha in Santiago de Chile.

# Conclusion

There is a growing concern about AI replacing humans, but in the world of humanities mapping, the worry should lessen for this year at least. While the AI layers were able to identify multiple places belonging to the story, the human layer was the only one to realistically map the road that Ross Goodwin portrayed in his book. Paradoxically, we might be looking at an early version of AI geoannotation, similar to I the Road’s primitive AI writing, which nowadays can sometimes match human writing. The question may then be about *when* AI will be able to match human geoannotation. However, I also question whether AI can ever actually match humans’ understanding of places, as it has never experienced them. Writing is ultimately composed of patterns of words, but space and time are very unique. Even as we try to reduce them to points, vectors, and data, they can never be fully accurate. Pascoe (2025) explores the idea of mapping in digital humanities, and also addresses human understanding of space and places. He explains that “understanding is only ever partial and provisional” (p. 208), and that a place has meaning through layers of cultures and experiences, which also involve how we interpret the place and how it interprets us (p. 209). As we explore places and give them meaning, even as humans, we are entering a new phase of us interpreting artificial intelligence and artificial intelligence giving us meaning too.
